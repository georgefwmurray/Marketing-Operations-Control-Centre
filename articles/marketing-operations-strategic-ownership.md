<h2>Start with the commercial objective</h2>
<p>Marketing operations is often judged on delivery: platforms managed, processes improved, campaigns supported. Those things matter, but at a certain level they are not enough. The real step up is taking ownership of the function's direction: deciding where to focus, where to invest and where to say no.</p>
<p>A marketing operations team should not exist simply to fulfil requests. Its priorities should come from what the business is trying to achieve.</p>
<p>In my role, that starts with the twice-yearly H1 and H2 planning cycle. We begin with the overall marketing target, account for seasonality and the previous year's performance, then split monthly lead targets across teams and lead sources using historical results. Each morning I review leads, workable leads and revenue against target with senior leaders, so we can see early where activity needs to change.</p>
<p>It also means challenging requests. When a proposal came forward to increase renewal communications, I mapped the customer lifecycle and identified email oversaturation. We reduced email volume by 17%. Click-through rates rose, without a negative impact on retention.</p>

<h2>Set a direction, not a delivery queue</h2>
<p>Without direction, an operations team becomes reactive. I use the priorities agreed with marketing leadership to shape a Digital Operations Plan, then translate them into team objectives and individual responsibilities. Progress is reviewed through performance dashboards, regular one-to-ones and structured objective reviews.</p>
<p>The point is that everyone can see not just what they are asked to deliver, but why it matters and how their work supports the wider marketing objectives.</p>

<h2>Make investment decisions based on value</h2>
<p>Technology decisions are often treated as buying decisions. I start with the underlying problem: is the capability genuinely missing, or is an existing platform underused?</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cancel what is not delivering value.</strong> An A/B testing and personalisation subscription was underused, while in-house WordPress development already covered much of its capability. I recommended cancelling it to the SVP of Marketing, secured approval and implemented the change. The budget was redirected towards central marketing activity, including campaigns and PPC.</li>
<li><strong>Consolidate where it makes sense.</strong> Following competitor analysis, I recommended moving a brand's marketing platform into our existing HubSpot environment. The assessment considered licence costs, internal expertise and training requirements. The SVP approved the recommendation.</li>
<li><strong>Rationalise the technology stack.</strong> A review identified five subscriptions that could be retired, avoiding unnecessary recurring costs.</li>
<li><strong>Reduce external dependency.</strong> Introducing our Content Agent reduced external content agency spend by 50%.</li>
</ul>
<p>The best decision is not always to spend less. Sometimes additional investment is justified. The test is being able to explain the problem, the options, the trade-offs and why the chosen approach represents the best use of resources.</p>

<h2>Lead change through people</h2>
<p>Process and technology changes rarely succeed through design alone. I developed an intern into a Digital Operations Executive, who now owns the continued development of our Data Import Agent. Agent ideas come from the people closest to the work, are refined together and then built by the team.</p>
<p>The aim is an operation where specialists own delivery, develop their expertise and have the confidence to make decisions within their areas of responsibility. I should not be the person every decision has to pass through.</p>

<h2>Measure contribution, not activity</h2>
<p>I look at commercial contribution, operational efficiency, technology value, team capability and reliability. Not every outcome can be credited to marketing operations alone, so I distinguish contribution from sole ownership.</p>
<p>I also keep confirmed spend reductions separate from estimated capacity and other provisional benefits. That distinction matters: a credible business case should make clear what has been saved, what capacity has been released and what remains an estimate. I explore this further in <a href="{{ '/insights/measuring-the-business-value-of-ai-automation/' | relative_url }}">How to measure the business value of AI automation</a>.</p>

<h2>Strategic ownership is ongoing</h2>
<p>Moving from delivery to strategic ownership does not mean stepping back from the detail. It means using that detail to decide when to challenge, when to invest, when to simplify and when to change direction.</p>
<p>Strategic ownership is about taking responsibility for the conditions that make good delivery possible: clear priorities, informed investment, capable people, effective processes and measurable outcomes.</p>
<p>The role of marketing operations leadership is to bring those elements together, ensuring the function does not simply respond to the business, but actively improves its ability to achieve its objectives.</p>
