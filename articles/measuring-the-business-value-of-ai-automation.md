  <h2>Start with the business problem, not the technology</h2>
  <p>AI automation has made it easier than ever to build workflows that analyse information, prepare content, check data and complete repeatable tasks. The temptation is to start with the technology: identify a new capability, build an agent and find somewhere to use it.</p>
  <p>The better approach is to start with the operational problem. Where is the team spending time on work that is repetitive, predictable or unnecessarily manual? Where do processes rely on someone remembering to complete a check? Where does inconsistent information slow down a decision?</p>
  <p>These are often better starting points than looking for opportunities to introduce AI for its own sake.</p>

  <h2>The five measures of automation value</h2>
  <p>Time saved is important, but it is only one part of the picture. I use five measures to understand the value of automation in a marketing operations environment:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Released capacity:</strong> time returned to the team that can be redirected towards higher-value work.</li>
    <li><strong>Direct cost reduction:</strong> external spend or subscription costs that can genuinely be removed.</li>
    <li><strong>Quality and consistency:</strong> fewer errors, more reliable processes and better adherence to standards.</li>
    <li><strong>Operational control:</strong> improved visibility, ownership, auditability and confidence in how work is completed.</li>
    <li><strong>Scalability:</strong> the ability to support more brands, campaigns or activity without increasing operational complexity at the same rate.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>These measures should not be treated as interchangeable. Released capacity is not automatically a cash saving. Avoiding a subscription renewal is a direct cost reduction. Preventing an error may have a financial benefit, but that benefit should be identified as an estimate unless it can be measured directly.</p>
  <p>The distinction matters when presenting results to leadership. A credible value model is more useful than a large headline number that combines different types of benefit without explaining them.</p>

  <h2>What this looks like in practice</h2>
  <p>In my own marketing operations environment, the Marketing Operations Control Centre (MOCC) has developed into a coordinated operating model supporting five brands. It brings together automation, reporting, quality control and governance rather than treating each workflow as a separate experiment.</p>
  <p>The current operation includes 11 live agents and releases an estimated 135 hours of team capacity each month. That is time that can be redirected towards work requiring judgement, commercial understanding and human input.</p>
  <p>There are also examples of direct financial impact. AI efficiency and bringing part of content production in-house reduced the amount of work outsourced to an agency. Monthly external content spend fell from £20,000 to £10,000, representing an annualised reduction of £120,000.</p>
  <p>Alongside this, five MarTech subscriptions were retired:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Wingify</strong> — website personalisation</li>
    <li><strong>Jasper</strong> — AI-assisted content production</li>
    <li><strong>ZeroBounce</strong> — email verification</li>
    <li><strong>BeeFree</strong> — email design and production</li>
    <li><strong>Clearbit</strong> — company and contact data enrichment</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Retiring these tools avoided approximately £14,000 in annual subscription spend through capability replacement and consolidation.</p>

  <h2>Putting a credible value against the results</h2>
  <p>Bringing the measures together gives a more complete view of the operational impact. The current model identifies approximately £198,700 in annual value:</p>
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th>Value category</th><th>Calculation or basis</th><th>Annual value</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Agency spend reduction</td><td>(£20,000 − £10,000) × 12 months</td><td>£120,000</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Subscription spend avoided</td><td>Combined annual cost of five retired tools</td><td>£14,000</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Released team capacity</td><td>135 hours × £25 blended hourly cost × 12 months</td><td>£40,500</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Rework prevention</td><td>Provisional estimate from the initial value assessment</td><td>£4,800</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Compliance and reliability</td><td>Provisional estimate from the initial value assessment</td><td>£19,400</td></tr>
      <tr><td><strong>Total annual value identified</strong></td><td></td><td><strong>£198,700</strong></td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <p>The calculations are deliberately presented at different levels of confidence. The agency reduction is calculated from the change in monthly external spend, annualised over 12 months. The subscription figure represents the combined annual cost of the five retired tools. The capacity valuation uses an illustrative blended internal cost of £25 per hour: 135 hours multiplied by £25 and 12 months gives £40,500. This is an indicative value of released time, not a payroll saving.</p>
  <p>The remaining £24,200 comprises two provisional placeholders carried forward from the initial MOCC value assessment: £4,800 for rework prevention and £19,400 for compliance and reliability. These figures have not been independently calculated from measured incident or rework baselines. They are included to identify potential operational benefits, not to represent verified cash savings. I would rather identify them honestly as provisional estimates than invent a calculation to make them appear more precise.</p>
  <p>That distinction is important. Of the £198,700 in annual value identified, £134,000 — approximately 67% — comes from reductions in external spend. The remaining £64,700 represents released team capacity and provisional operational estimates. These categories are deliberately separated: not all identified value is cash saved, and presenting them as equivalent would overstate the financial impact.</p>
  <p>The purpose of a value model is not to make every benefit look like a financial saving. It is to give leadership an honest view of what has changed, what it is worth and how confidently that value can be measured.</p>

  <h2>Released capacity only matters if you use it well</h2>
  <p>One of the easiest mistakes in automation is to stop measuring once a task takes less time.</p>
  <p>If an automation saves someone two hours a week but the business does nothing differently with those two hours, the benefit may be limited. The opportunity comes from deciding where that capacity should go next.</p>
  <p>That might mean spending more time improving campaign performance, strengthening data quality, supporting commercial planning or addressing work that previously had to wait. It might also mean allowing a team to manage a larger or more complex operation without adding the same level of resource.</p>
  <p>Automation creates the opportunity; management decisions determine whether the business captures the value.</p>

  <h2>Don't automate a broken process</h2>
  <p>Automation can make a good process more efficient, but it can also make a bad process faster and harder to understand.</p>
  <p>Before building, establish whether the process is clear, whether the inputs are reliable, who owns the outcome and what should happen when something goes wrong. If those questions cannot be answered, the first task is usually to improve the process rather than automate it.</p>
  <p>Bringing part of content production in-house using AI is a good example. AI can accelerate drafting and repeatable production steps, but it does not remove the need for clear briefs, brand standards and human quality checks. Without those foundations, automation can simply move rework further down the process.</p>
  <p>This is particularly important when AI agents are involved. They need defined permissions, appropriate human review, a clear failure path and someone accountable for their operation. Without those controls, apparent efficiency can create a different kind of operational cost.</p>

  <h2>Build an operating model that captures the value</h2>
  <p>The biggest opportunity is not building the largest possible collection of agents. It is creating an operating environment in which people, processes, data and technology work together.</p>
  <p>For every automation or operational improvement, I would record the original problem, the baseline, what changed, the measured result, the value category and the person accountable for the outcome. I would also revisit the figures regularly: usage changes, processes evolve and tools that once delivered value can become unnecessary.</p>
  <p>That means having a clear view of what is automated, what remains a human responsibility, how performance is monitored and how improvements are prioritised. It also means developing capability within the team so that automation does not become dependent on one person or technical specialist.</p>

  <h2>What I would tell another marketing leader</h2>
  <p>Start with a business problem, establish a baseline, measure the result in the right category and give someone ownership of the outcome. Be honest about what is a cash saving and what is an estimate. Make sure the capacity you release is put to better use.</p>
  <p>Don't judge success by the number of automations or agents you have deployed. Judge it by whether the business is operating better.</p>
