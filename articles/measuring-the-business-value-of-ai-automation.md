<h2>Start with the business problem, not the technology</h2>
<p>AI automation makes it easier to analyse information, prepare content, check data and complete repeatable tasks. The temptation is to start with a new capability, build an agent and then look for somewhere to use it.</p>
<p>A better approach is to start with the operational problem. Where is the team spending time on repetitive or unnecessarily manual work? Where do processes rely on someone remembering to complete a check? Where does inconsistent information slow down a decision?</p>
<p>These are often better starting points than introducing AI for its own sake.</p>

<h2>Five measures of automation value</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Released capacity:</strong> time returned to the team that can be redirected towards higher-value work.</li>
<li><strong>Direct cost reduction:</strong> external spend or subscription costs that can genuinely be removed.</li>
<li><strong>Quality and consistency:</strong> fewer errors, more reliable processes and better adherence to standards.</li>
<li><strong>Operational control:</strong> improved visibility, ownership, auditability and confidence in how work is completed.</li>
<li><strong>Scalability:</strong> the ability to support more brands, campaigns or activity without increasing operational complexity at the same rate.</li>
</ol>
<p>These measures are not interchangeable. Released capacity is not automatically a cash saving. A cancelled subscription is a direct cost reduction. Preventing an error may have financial value, but that benefit should remain an estimate unless it is measured against a reliable baseline.</p>

<h2>What this looks like in practice</h2>
<p>I proposed and led the development of a Marketing Operations Control Centre (MOCC) for a B2B SaaS business operating across five brands. The aim was to move beyond isolated experiments and establish a coordinated operating model for quality, production, data, insight and governance.</p>
<p>The programme also contributed to a 50% reduction in external content spend by changing how content work was produced and supported. The important point is not a headline figure in isolation; it is the combination of a clear business problem, a change to the operating model and a measurable commercial outcome.</p>
<p>Further implementation detail and supporting financial evidence are reserved for relevant employer conversations. This public example deliberately avoids presenting internal cost data or estimates as independently verified savings.</p>

<h2>Separate cash savings from capacity value</h2>
<p>When presenting automation to leadership, report direct reductions in external spend separately from time released to the team. If capacity is valued using an hourly rate, label it as an illustrative capacity value rather than a cash saving. Any estimates for rework, compliance or reliability should be shown separately, with their assumptions and confidence level made clear.</p>
<p>A useful report should make it possible to answer four questions:</p>
<ul><li>What spend has actually left the budget?</li><li>What work can the team now complete with the time released?</li><li>What quality or control improvements can be evidenced?</li><li>Which benefits remain estimates and what would validate them?</li></ul>

<h2>Make the value model useful to decision-makers</h2>
<p>Leadership does not need the biggest possible number. It needs a clear view of what has changed, why it matters, how it is measured and how confident the organisation can be in the result.</p>
<p>That discipline makes it easier to compare opportunities, prioritise investment and decide whether an automation should be scaled, improved or retired.</p>

<h2>The principle</h2>
<p>Automate work where there is a real operational case. Measure the impact in the right category. Keep human accountability visible. And never combine cash savings, released capacity and provisional estimates into one headline that suggests they are the same thing.</p>
