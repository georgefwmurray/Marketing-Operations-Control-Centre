<p>Marketing Operations is often seen as the function that keeps marketing systems running: managing the CRM, building automation, maintaining data and producing reports. That describes what it does, not what it is for.</p>
<p>A high-performing Marketing Operations function understands the business, translates commercial priorities into operational capability, and gives marketing the structure, data and technology to deliver measurable results. It is judged not by how much it manages, but by how much better marketing performs because it exists.</p>
<p>That requires a different way of thinking about the function: not simply as a technical support team, but as an operational and strategic partner to marketing leadership.</p>

<h2>1. Start with business priorities, not technology</h2>
<p>One of the biggest mistakes an organisation can make is defining its Marketing Operations strategy around the technology it owns.</p>
<p>A new CRM, marketing automation platform or AI tool might create opportunities, but technology alone doesn't determine what the business needs to achieve.</p>
<p>The starting point should be commercial priorities.</p>
<p>Is the business trying to generate more qualified demand? Improve conversion? Increase customer retention? Support expansion into new markets? Bring multiple brands or teams into a more consistent operating model?</p>
<p>Marketing Operations should translate those priorities into the capabilities, processes and measures needed to support them.</p>
<p>That means being involved early in planning, rather than being brought in once someone has already decided what needs to be built.</p>
<p>It also means being prepared to challenge requests. Not every problem needs another platform, automation or dashboard. Sometimes the answer is a clearer process, better ownership or a more consistent definition of success.</p>

<h2>2. Build an operating model people can work within</h2>
<p>A strategy only becomes useful when people understand how to put it into practice.</p>
<p>A strong Marketing Operations function establishes the operating model that connects marketing teams, activities and responsibilities.</p>
<p>This includes:</p>
<ul>
<li>Clear ownership of processes, platforms and performance.</li>
<li>Consistent definitions for leads, lifecycle stages and qualification.</li>
<li>Agreed planning and prioritisation processes.</li>
<li>Defined responsibilities between Marketing, Sales and other teams.</li>
<li>Practical workflows that reduce unnecessary friction.</li>
<li>Governance that supports consistency without slowing everything down.</li>
</ul>
<p>This becomes particularly important in multi-brand or multi-region organisations.</p>
<p>Different teams may have different audiences, propositions and commercial objectives. They still need a shared operational foundation: reliable data, consistent processes, clear accountability and visibility of performance.</p>
<p>The objective isn't to make every team work in exactly the same way. It's to create enough consistency to operate effectively while allowing the flexibility the business needs.</p>

<h2>3. Connect marketing activity to commercial performance</h2>
<p>Reporting on activity is not the same as understanding performance.</p>
<p>Campaign volumes, email engagement, website traffic and lead generation figures can all be useful. But they become much more valuable when connected to what happens next.</p>
<p>A high-performing Marketing Operations function helps answer questions such as:</p>
<ul>
<li>Are we generating leads that Sales can genuinely work?</li>
<li>How quickly are leads being followed up?</li>
<li>Where are opportunities progressing or stalling?</li>
<li>Which channels and activities are contributing to pipeline?</li>
<li>Where are we losing potential revenue?</li>
<li>Are marketing investments producing the intended return?</li>
</ul>
<p>This requires a joined-up view of the lead lifecycle, from initial engagement through qualification, sales acceptance and revenue.</p>
<p>It also requires Marketing and Sales to agree on definitions and responsibilities. If teams use different criteria for what constitutes a qualified lead, or have no shared understanding of follow-up expectations, reporting becomes unreliable and opportunities can be missed.</p>
<p>The value of Marketing Operations is not simply producing another dashboard. It is creating the visibility and accountability that allow leaders to act on what the data is telling them.</p>

<h2>4. Treat MarTech as a strategic portfolio</h2>
<p>Marketing technology should support the operating model, not dictate it.</p>
<p>As organisations grow, their technology environments often become increasingly complex. New platforms are introduced to solve individual problems, integrations accumulate and overlapping capabilities emerge.</p>
<p>Without clear ownership and direction, the result can be a fragmented technology stack that is expensive to maintain and difficult for teams to use effectively.</p>
<p>A strategic approach to MarTech considers:</p>
<ul>
<li>Whether existing platforms support business priorities.</li>
<li>Where capabilities overlap or important gaps exist.</li>
<li>How data moves between systems.</li>
<li>Whether integrations are reliable and maintainable.</li>
<li>What should be improved, consolidated, replaced or retired.</li>
<li>Whether teams have the skills and processes to get value from the investment.</li>
</ul>
<p>This is where Marketing Operations moves beyond platform administration and into technology strategy.</p>
<p>The question changes from <em>What can this platform do?</em> to <em>What does the business need, and what is the best way to deliver it?</em></p>
<p>That distinction matters. It encourages better investment decisions and helps ensure technology remains an enabler of performance rather than a source of unnecessary complexity.</p>

<h2>5. Build capability, not dependency</h2>
<p>A Marketing Operations function should not become a bottleneck where every decision, request or technical task depends on one person.</p>
<p>As the function develops, its focus should shift towards building capability across the team.</p>
<p>That means establishing clear responsibilities, developing technical and commercial understanding, documenting important processes and giving people ownership of outcomes.</p>
<p>It also means creating effective relationships with the wider marketing organisation.</p>
<p>Marketing Operations needs to understand the challenges faced by campaign, content, digital, demand generation and lifecycle teams. Those teams, in turn, need to understand how operational decisions affect their work.</p>
<p>The strongest functions don't simply deliver services to the rest of marketing. They help the wider team become more capable and effective.</p>
<p>For leaders, this means investing in people as deliberately as they invest in platforms and processes.</p>

<h2>6. Use automation and AI with purpose and accountability</h2>
<p>Automation has long been part of Marketing Operations. AI is now expanding what can be achieved, from improving data processes and quality assurance to supporting analysis, content workflows and operational decision-making.</p>
<p>But introducing AI does not automatically create a better operating model.</p>
<p>The starting point should still be the business problem.</p>
<p>Where is the team spending time on repetitive work? Where are delays or errors occurring? Which processes could benefit from more consistent analysis? Where could AI improve capacity without compromising quality or control?</p>
<p>A responsible approach also needs clear ownership, testing, human oversight and governance.</p>
<p>Not every process should be automated, and not every AI-generated output should be trusted without review.</p>
<p>The objective is not to maximise the number of tools or agents in use. It is to improve how work gets done, release capacity for higher-value activity and make the function more effective as it scales.</p>

<h2>7. Measure Marketing Operations' own contribution</h2>
<p>Marketing Operations should be able to demonstrate its value, just as it expects other marketing functions to demonstrate theirs.</p>
<p>That means looking beyond the number of campaigns supported, workflows built or tickets completed.</p>
<p>Useful measures might include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Improvements in lead quality and conversion.</li>
<li>Reduced time between lead generation and sales follow-up.</li>
<li>Better visibility of pipeline and revenue contribution.</li>
<li>Increased adoption and effective use of marketing technology.</li>
<li>Reduced duplication, manual effort and operational cost.</li>
<li>Improved data quality and process reliability.</li>
<li>Greater team capacity for strategic and creative work.</li>
</ul>
<p>The right measures will vary by organisation. What matters is that they reflect the outcomes the function is there to support.</p>
<p>Marketing Operations should be able to explain not just what it delivered, but why it mattered.</p>

<h2>A practical example: measuring the impact of an AI-enabled operating model</h2>
<p>The principles above are not theoretical. In my own Marketing Operations Control Centre work, the focus has been on changing the operating model and measuring what improves, rather than counting automations.</p>
<p>One established commercial measure was a <strong>50% reduction in external content spend</strong>, achieved by changing the balance between internal capability and external production.</p>
<p>At workflow level, one repeatable onboarding-asset preparation process reduced from around <strong>two hours to around 20 minutes</strong>. That is a reduction in preparation time for that specific workflow, not a claim that every asset or activity takes the same amount of time. Outputs still go through human review before they are used.</p>
<p>These measures describe different kinds of value. The spend reduction is a direct cost outcome; the shorter preparation time is operational capacity released. They should be reported separately rather than combined into an assumed financial saving.</p>

<h2>So what does a high-performing function look like?</h2>
<p>It is not necessarily the organisation with the largest technology stack, the most automation or the biggest team.</p>
<p>It is the function that understands the business, translates strategy into operational priorities and gives marketing the structure and capability to deliver.</p>
<p>It connects people, processes, data and technology. It creates clarity around ownership and performance. It makes informed decisions about investment and continually looks for ways to improve how marketing operates.</p>
<p>Most importantly, it has a seat at the table before decisions are made, not just when someone needs something implemented.</p>
<p>That is the difference between Marketing Operations as a delivery function and Marketing Operations as a strategic capability.</p>
<p>The ultimate measure of success is not how much operational activity the function manages. It is how much better the marketing organisation performs because the function exists.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>What is Marketing Operations?</h3>
<p>Marketing Operations is the function that connects marketing strategy to the people, processes, data and technology needed to deliver it. It covers the operating model, the MarTech stack, lead lifecycle and reporting, so that marketing activity can be executed consistently and measured against commercial outcomes.</p>

<h3>What does a high-performing Marketing Operations function do differently?</h3>
<p>It starts with business priorities rather than technology, and it is involved in planning before decisions are made. It also defines clear ownership and shared definitions with Sales, and connects activity to pipeline and revenue. It is willing to challenge requests that don't solve a real problem.</p>

<h3>How should Marketing Operations approach AI and automation?</h3>
<p>Start with a specific business problem, such as repetitive work, delays or inconsistent quality, rather than the tool. Put clear ownership, testing and human oversight in place, and review AI-generated output before relying on it. The aim is to free capacity for higher-value work, not to maximise the number of tools or agents in use.</p>

<h3>How do you measure the value of Marketing Operations?</h3>
<p>Look at outcomes rather than volume of work. Useful measures include lead quality and conversion, speed of sales follow-up, pipeline visibility, technology adoption, reduced manual effort and cost, data quality, and the capacity released for strategic work.</p>
